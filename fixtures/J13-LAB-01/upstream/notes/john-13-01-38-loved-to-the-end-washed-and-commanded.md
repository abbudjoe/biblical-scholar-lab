# John 13:1–38: Loved to the End, Washed, and Commanded

## Reading context

John 13 opens the final meal and the farewell discourse. Jesus knows that his hour has come, that he is leaving the world for the Father, that the Father has placed all things into his hands, that he came from God, and that he is returning to God.

Within that full knowledge of his authority, origin, destination, betrayal, and approaching death, Jesus rises from supper, removes his garments, takes a towel, and washes his disciples' feet.

The chapter then moves through Peter's refusal, Jesus' insistence that Peter must receive the washing, the distinction between bathing and washing, the command to imitate Jesus' service, Judas's betrayal, the language of glory, the new commandment to love as Jesus loved, and Peter's confident promise followed by Jesus' prediction of denial.

John 13 is the immediate doorway into John 14–16. The promise, dwelling, belief, remaining, fruit, pruning, persecution, and Spirit language of the following chapters is addressed to disciples who have just been washed by Jesus, warned of betrayal, commanded to love, and told that their strongest spokesman will deny him.

## Central questions

The chapter raises several connected questions:

> Why must Peter receive Jesus' washing in order to have a share with him?

> Why does Jesus turn a concrete act of footwashing into an obligatory pattern for his followers?

> How can betrayal and the approaching cross become the moment Jesus describes as glory?

> Why does Peter imagine that discipleship begins with laying down his life for Jesus when the chapter has shown that Jesus must serve, wash, and lay down his life first?

## Chapter structure

```text
John 13:1-5
Jesus loves his own to the end and takes the servant's place.

John 13:6-11
Peter resists the washing, but Jesus makes receiving it necessary for participation with him.

John 13:12-17
Jesus explains the act as a pattern his servants are obligated to practice.

John 13:18-30
The table companion is identified as the betrayer, Satan enters Judas, and Judas goes into the night.

John 13:31-35
The betrayal begins the hour Jesus calls glory, and Jesus gives the new commandment to love as he has loved.

John 13:36-38
Peter promises to lay down his life for Jesus, but Jesus predicts that Peter will deny him three times.
```

## Detailed close reading

### John 13:1: love **εἰς τέλος**

John says that Jesus:

> **εἰς τέλος ἠγάπησεν αὐτούς**

The phrase **εἰς τέλος** (*eis telos*) is commonly translated “to the end.” It can be heard temporally, as love continuing to the end, and the context also supports the effect of love brought to completion or fullest expression.

The grammar doesn't require both senses in every use. In this narrative, however, Jesus' love continues through betrayal, denial, abandonment, suffering, and death, while the footwashing and cross display what that love does.

### John 13:2: during supper or after supper

The KJV reads “supper being ended,” while the ASV and WEB read “during supper.”

The difference reflects a textual variant:

```text
γινομένου
while it was happening, during

γενομένου
having happened, after it occurred
```

Modern critical editions generally print **γινομένου**, the reading behind “during supper.” This is a manuscript question, not simply a translator choosing a smoother English phrase.

The central theological movement remains intact under either reading, but the timing of the footwashing within the meal differs.

### John 13:3-5: authority expressed through service

John repeats that Jesus knows:

```text
his hour has come
he is returning to the Father
all things are in his hands
he came from God
he is going to God
```

Then Jesus uses those hands to wash the disciples' feet.

He doesn't serve because he has forgotten his status. He serves while fully conscious that he is Lord, that all things have been entrusted to him, and that he is returning to God.

Divine authority doesn't prevent lowly service in this passage. It is expressed through it.

The Greek says he laid aside his **ἱμάτια**, “garments.” Versions that say “outer garments” clarify what the scene most naturally implies, but “outer” isn't a separate Greek word.

### Laying aside and taking up

The sequence is:

```text
Jesus lays aside his garments.
Jesus descends into the servant's task.
Jesus washes and cleanses.
Jesus takes his garments again.
Jesus returns to his place.
```

John 10 uses related language when Jesus says he lays down his life and takes it again. The objects differ, garments in John 13 and life in John 10, so the scenes shouldn't be called identical wordplay. Still, the enacted sequence may anticipate Jesus' larger self-giving movement.

Jesus tells Peter that he doesn't understand now but will understand afterward, which suggests that the footwashing's meaning reaches beyond immediate hygiene.

### John 13:6: “Lord, are **you** washing my feet?”

Peter says:

> **Κύριε, σύ μου νίπτεις τοὺς πόδας;**

The explicit pronoun **σύ**, “you,” makes the reversal visible. Peter's shock isn't that his feet are being washed. It is that Jesus is doing it.

Source handle: `SP01-SRC-002#John.13.6:σύ`

### John 13:8: Peter's emphatic refusal

Peter says:

> **Οὐ μὴ νίψῃς μου τοὺς πόδας εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα.**

The combined negative construction and the phrase “for the age” make the refusal exceptionally strong:

> You will certainly never wash my feet. Not ever.

Peter may think he is honoring Jesus by refusing to let his Lord stoop before him. Jesus treats the refusal as rejection of something Peter must receive.

Source handle: `SP01-SRC-002#John.13.8:οὐ_μὴ_νίψῃς`

### John 13:8: “no share with me”

Jesus answers:

> **Ἐὰν μὴ νίψω σε, οὐκ ἔχεις μέρος μετʼ ἐμοῦ.**

The noun **μέρος** (*meros*) can mean part, share, portion, or participation.

Translations include:

```text
no part with me
no share with me
nothing in common with me
```

“Nothing in common” makes a relational effect explicit but may narrow the phrase. “No share with me” better preserves participation in Jesus' fellowship, life, and destiny without deciding every theological implication.

Peter can't establish participation by protecting Jesus from humiliation. He must receive what Jesus does for him.

Source handle: `SP01-SRC-002#John.13.8:μέρος`

### John 13:9-10: bathing and washing

Peter moves from total refusal to total excess. He asks Jesus to wash his feet, hands, and head.

Jesus distinguishes two verbs:

```text
λελουμένος
from λούω
one who has bathed

νίψασθαι
from νίπτω
to wash a particular body part, here the feet
```

English versions that use “wash” for both verbs can hide the contrast. “The one who has bathed only needs to wash the feet” preserves it.

The perfect participle **λελουμένος** presents the person as one who has undergone a bath and remains in the resulting condition. The localized footwashing is then distinguished from that completed bath.

The grammar doesn't by itself settle whether the bath represents baptism, regeneration, initial cleansing, participation in Jesus' death, or another theological category.

Source handles:

- `SP01-SRC-002#John.13.10:λελουμένος`
- `SP01-SRC-002#John.13.10:νίψασθαι`

### John 13:10: “except the feet”

The words represented by “except the feet” are absent from some manuscripts. Most modern critical texts retain them, but the variant should be verified against an admitted critical apparatus before it is treated as frozen source data.

Textual-variant status: pending apparatus verification.

### John 13:10-11: clean, but not all

Jesus says the bathed person is completely clean, then tells the disciples:

> You are clean, but not all.

John explains that Jesus says this because he knows who will betray him.

The passage has moved beyond hygiene. Judas's problem isn't that he needs a more thorough physical bath. Cleanness has become a relational, moral, or spiritual category connected with reception of Jesus and betrayal of him.

John 15:3 later says:

> You are already clean because of the word I have spoken to you.

This creates a strong connection between Jesus' washing, Jesus' word, cleansing, and the later command to remain.

Source handles:

- `SP01-SRC-002#John.13.10:καθαροί`
- `SP01-SRC-001#John.15.3`

### Did Jesus wash Judas's feet?

Judas is present in the narrative before the washing and leaves later. Jesus speaks after washing “their feet,” without narrating an exception, while also saying that not all are clean.

It is reasonable to infer that Judas was present for and may have received the footwashing. John never individually says, “Jesus washed Judas's feet,” so the point should remain an inference.

The scene at minimum presents Jesus serving in full knowledge that his betrayer is among them.

### John 13:12-15: Teacher, Lord, obligation, and pattern

Jesus asks whether the disciples understand what he has done.

They call him:

```text
διδάσκαλος
Teacher

κύριος
Lord
```

The KJV's “Master” reflects older English, but **διδάσκαλος** directly means “Teacher.” Jesus affirms both titles rather than rejecting hierarchy.

He then argues from his lordship:

> If I, the Lord and Teacher, washed your feet, you also **ought** to wash one another's feet.

The verb **ὀφείλετε** means that they owe it or are obligated. This isn't merely an invitation to admire humility.

Jesus says he has given them an **ὑπόδειγμα**, an example, pattern, model, or demonstration. “Example” can sound optional in modern English, but the preceding obligation gives the model continuing force.

### Literal footwashing and the larger pattern

Jesus gives a concrete command to wash one another's feet and identifies his act as a pattern.

Christian traditions differ over whether this institutes a continuing ordinance or primarily establishes a wider pattern of humble service. The passage supports both a concrete act and a broader form of discipleship.

The safest synthesis is:

> Jesus gives a concrete act to imitate and, through that act, a larger pattern of self-lowering love.

### John 13:16: servant, slave, and sent one

Jesus says:

> A **δοῦλος** isn't greater than his lord, nor an **ἀπόστολος** greater than the one who sent him.

**δοῦλος** can be translated “servant” or “slave.” “Slave” makes the status difference more explicit.

**ἀπόστολος** here has its ordinary sense of “one who is sent” or “messenger.” It needn't be limited to the formal title “Apostle.”

The point is that no servant or messenger can claim exemption from the lowly service practiced by the Lord and sender.

Source handles:

- `SP01-SRC-002#John.13.16:δοῦλος`
- `SP01-SRC-002#John.13.16:ἀπόστολος`

### John 13:17: knowing and doing

Jesus says:

> If you know these things, **μακάριοί** are you if you do them.

The KJV says “happy.” Modern translations commonly use “blessed.” **μακάριοι** describes a favored or flourishing condition, not merely a passing emotion.

The blessing is attached to doing, not only knowing.

This gives the philological study its own warning. A reader may understand **εἰς τέλος**, distinguish **λούω** from **νίπτω**, explain **μέρος**, and parse **ὑπόδειγμα**, while refusing the life the passage requires.

Source handle: `SP01-SRC-002#John.13.17:μακάριοι`

### John 13:18: lifting the heel

Jesus quotes the image:

> He who eats bread with me has lifted up his heel against me.

Some translations retain the physical image. Others explain it as turning against or betraying Jesus.

The explanatory renderings communicate hostility but lose the scriptural body metaphor. The image is especially painful because the betrayer is a table companion.

Source handles:

- `SP01-SRC-002#John.13.18:πτέρναν`
- `SP01-SRC-004#Psalm.41.9`

### John 13:19: “I am he” or “I am”

The Greek ends:

> **ὅτι ἐγώ εἰμι**

There is no separate Greek word corresponding to “he.” English versions often supply it to complete the sentence naturally:

> that I am he

Leaving the phrase as “that I am” makes John's repeated **ἐγώ εἰμι** formula more visible.

The wording may carry divine-name resonance within John's wider Gospel, but grammar alone doesn't prove that every use is a direct quotation of Exodus 3:14. Here, Jesus' foreknowledge of betrayal is meant to strengthen belief in his identity when the prediction occurs.

Source handle: `SP01-SRC-002#John.13.19:ἐγώ_εἰμι`

### John 13:23-25: bosom, chest, and reclining beside Jesus

The meal was eaten in a reclining posture. The beloved disciple was beside Jesus and could lean back toward his chest to speak privately.

Translations include:

```text
in Jesus' bosom
leaning against Jesus' chest
reclining at Jesus' side
```

“Bosom” preserves intimate body language but sounds unusual in modern English. “At his side” is natural but may hide the physical closeness of the scene.

Source handles:

- `SP01-SRC-002#John.13.23:κόλπῳ`
- `SP01-SRC-002#John.13.25:στῆθος`

### John 13:26: sop, morsel, or piece of bread

The noun **ψωμίον** means a small piece or morsel of bread.

The KJV's “sop” refers to a dipped morsel but is now unfamiliar. “Morsel” preserves the small-piece idea. “Piece of bread” is clearest.

The act of dipping and handing the morsel identifies Judas. Any claim that the gesture was definitively a formal sign of honor requires historical evidence beyond the noun itself.

Source handle: `SP01-SRC-002#John.13.26:ψωμίον`

### John 13:27-30: Satan, departure, and night

After Judas receives the morsel, Satan enters him. Judas leaves immediately, and John says:

> It was night.

The sentence certainly gives the time. Because John repeatedly uses light and darkness imagery, the line may also carry narrative resonance. The noun **νύξ**, “night,” doesn't establish that symbolism by itself, so the symbolic reading should remain interpretive.

Source handle: `SP01-SRC-002#John.13.30:νύξ`

### John 13:31-32: glory begins with betrayal

After Judas leaves, Jesus says:

> Now the Son of Man **ἐδοξάσθη**.

The form **ἐδοξάσθη** is an aorist passive indicative. English translations use “is glorified,” “was glorified,” or “has been glorified.”

The betrayal and cross are still ahead, but the hour has decisively begun. The aorist can present the glorification as inaugurated or treated as a decisive whole, without implying that every stage of cross, resurrection, and exaltation is already chronologically complete.

Source handle: `SP01-SRC-002#John.13.31:ἐδοξάσθη`

### John 13:32 textual variant

The KJV includes:

> If God is glorified in him...

Some major early manuscripts omit that conditional clause, and many modern critical editions move directly to God glorifying the Son.

Textual-variant status: pending verification against an admitted critical apparatus.

### John 13:34-35: the new commandment

Jesus gives:

> **ἐντολὴν καινὴν**
> a new commandment

The command to love wasn't new in the sense that Scripture had never commanded love. The immediate context gives it a new defining standard:

> as I have loved you

The footwashing has enacted that love, and the cross will carry it to completion.

The newness shouldn't be made to rest on a lexical slogan that **καινός** always means “new in quality rather than time.” The narrative explains the newness more securely than that simplification does.

Love becomes the public mark by which people recognize Jesus' disciples.

Source handle: `SP01-SRC-002#John.13.34:ἐντολὴν_καινήν`

### John 13:36-38: Peter will lay down his life, but not first

Peter says:

> I will lay down my life for you.

Jesus predicts that Peter will deny him three times before the rooster crows.

Peter imagines discipleship beginning with his heroic act for Jesus. The chapter has established the opposite order:

```text
Jesus serves Peter.
Jesus washes Peter.
Jesus cleanses Peter.
Jesus lays down his life first.
Peter receives before he follows.
```

Peter will later follow and suffer, but not as the independent hero or source of his own faithfulness.

## How John 13 prepares John 14 and 15

### Cleansing before remaining

```text
John 13:
Unless I wash you, you have no share with me.

John 15:
You are already clean because of my word.
Remain in me.
```

Jesus cleanses before he commands the disciples to remain and bear fruit.

### Love before command

John 13 gives the new commandment:

> Love one another as I have loved you.

The footwashing has already shown the form of that love. John 15 repeats the same standard and connects it with laying down one's life for friends.

### Betrayal, glory, and troubled hearts

Judas leaves, Satan's work advances, and the hour of apparent defeat begins. Jesus calls that hour glory.

Peter promises loyalty and receives a prediction of denial. Immediately after that prediction, John 14 begins:

> Don't let your heart be troubled. Believe in God. Believe also in me.

John 14's comfort is therefore addressed to disciples whose community is being torn by betrayal, whose leader will deny Jesus, and who can't follow Jesus where he is going.

### Jesus lays down his life before Peter can

Peter says he will lay down his life for Jesus. Jesus will lay down his life first.

The order throughout the chapter is:

```text
Jesus serves first.
Jesus washes first.
Jesus cleanses first.
Jesus loves first.
Jesus lays down his life first.
The disciple receives before imitating.
```

## Interpretive limits

The current evidence doesn't by itself establish:

- that **εἰς τέλος** must encode two simultaneous lexical meanings in every context;
- one final theological identification of the completed bath and localized footwashing;
- that the footwashing must be practiced only as a recurring ordinance or only as a broad metaphor;
- that Judas definitely received the washing, although the narrative makes the inference reasonable;
- that the laying aside and taking up of garments is an intentional direct symbol of death and resurrection rather than a literary echo or enacted anticipation;
- that “it was night” is only symbolic rather than literal time;
- that every occurrence of **ἐγώ εἰμι** is a direct divine-name quotation;
- that the dipped morsel was definitively a formal gesture of honor;
- that the aorist in **ἐδοξάσθη** means the complete glorification sequence had already occurred chronologically;
- the exact manuscript support for the variants in verses 2, 10, and 32 before an apparatus is admitted;
- that **καινός** alone explains what makes the commandment new;
- that knowing the Greek fulfills the command without obedient practice.

## Strongest synthesis

Jesus loves his disciples to the end by taking the servant's place and performing a washing they must receive if they are to have a share with him.

Peter initially resists because he can't accept a Lord who stoops to serve him. Jesus insists that participation begins with being washed by him, not with proving devotion to him.

Once the disciples receive Jesus' cleansing service, they are obligated to extend the same self-lowering love to one another.

The chapter then places betrayal, glory, love, and denial beside one another. Judas turns against Jesus. Jesus says the hour of glory has begun. Jesus commands love shaped by his own action. Peter promises to die for Jesus and is told that he will deny him.

The order is the point:

```text
Grace comes before imitation.
Cleansing comes before service.
Receiving Jesus' love comes before loving as he loved.
Jesus lays down his life before Peter can lay down his.
```

The deepest question is:

> Am I willing not only to serve Jesus, but to let Jesus serve, expose, and cleanse me on his terms?
